Will My Insurance Cover This? It Depends on the Paper Trail

A sudden, accidental event — a pipe that bursts without warning — is typically covered under a standard homeowner's policy. Gradual leaks, groundwater seepage and sewage backups are usually excluded unless you carry separate coverage. Most denied claims are not really arguments about what happened; they are arguments about what can be proven. Documentation from day one is the best protection for your claim.

Denial reason one: "It looks gradual"

An adjuster who sees a cleaned-up room and a stain cannot tell a burst pipe from a three-month seep. Photographs taken before mitigation — the failed pipe, the standing water, the spread — are what place the loss on the sudden side of the line. This is why the order matters: document first, then clean.

Denial reason two: no readings, no record

Insurers ask for moisture readings, a cause assessment, and a log of what was done and when. A job documented this way — photos, moisture readings, cause assessment, in a format built for insurance claims — answers those questions as a matter of routine. A verbal "we dried it" does not.

Denial reason three: the DIY gap

Cleaning it up yourself feels responsible, and for a small, immediately caught spill it is fine. But once water has soaked into flooring, drywall or subfloor, home cleanup usually only dries the surface while moisture stays trapped underneath — exactly what leads to mold weeks later — and undocumented work can complicate the claim itself. If it might be a claim, treat it like one from the first hour.

What a documented job includes

Phone triage records what is flooding, where, and how long it has been sitting. The on-site inspection logs moisture levels across walls, floors and subflooring. Extraction, drying and any remediation are photographed and logged as they happen, and moisture readings are rechecked at the end before the job closes. Coordination with your adjuster can run directly off that file.
